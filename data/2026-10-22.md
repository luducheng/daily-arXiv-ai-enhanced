### [1] [Direct Injection of Chemically-Enhanced Plasma into a Solar Flare Arcade](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28961)
*Jeffrey W. Reep,Andy S. H. To*

Main category: astro-ph.SR

TL;DR: Direct injection of reconnection-exhaust plasma near flare-loop apices can reproduce the magnitude and spatial spread of low-FIP abundance enhancements observed in the gradual phase of an X11.9 flare, and chromospheric heating narrows the enhancement to bright knots near arcade tops.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: To et al. (2024) proposed that the reconnection exhaust in standard solar flares directly injects coronal-abundance plasma onto the newly formed flare arcade from the apex downward, potentially explaining post-flare loop abundance distributions. This work tests that proposal directly.

Method: The authors model the spatiotemporal variation of low-FIP elements and inject material directly near loop apices, examining how the injected plasma spreads across the corona and how chromospheric heating affects the spatial extent of the low-FIP enhancement.

Result: Direct injection produces low-FIP enhancements comparable in magnitude and spatial spread across the corona to those observed during the gradual phase of an X11.9 flare. Strong chromospheric heating confines the enhancement to a narrow region near the injection site, and the low-FIP enhancement naturally accounts for the bright knots commonly seen near the top of flare arcades.

Conclusion: The direct-injection scenario is consistent with observed post-flare loop abundance patterns and offers a natural explanation for apex bright knots, supporting the reconnection-exhaust interpretation of flare-arcade composition.

Abstract: The magnetic reconnection event that drives "standard" solar flares ejects material from the reconnection site, loading mass directly onto the newly-reconnected flare arcade. To et al (2024) suggested that this model can explain observations of the distribution of elemental abundances measured in post-flare loops: the reconnection exhaust directly injects plasma with coronal abundances onto the arcade, flowing from the apex downwards. We test this idea directly by modeling the spatiotemporal variation of elements with low first ionization potential (FIP), and directly inject material near loop apices. We show that this direct injection is capable of producing enhancements similar in magnitude and spatial spread across the corona to those observed during the gradual phase of an X11.9 flare. We also show that direct chromospheric heating strongly limits the width of the low-FIP enhancement to a narrow region near the injection site. The enhancement of low-FIP elemental abundances naturally explains bright knots commonly observed near the top of flare arcades.

### [2] [Rotation in the Lagoon Nebula with K2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29006)
*L. M. Rebull,A. M. Cody,L. Venuti,S. Thanvantri,A. Thanvantri*

Main category: astro-ph.SR

TL;DR: The paper reports rotation periods from K2 light curves of roughly 1–2 Myr-old stars in the Lagoon Nebula cluster.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: A low-mass star's rotation rate drives its magnetic field and, for young stars, influences accretion and planetary formation, so observed rotation rates of young stars constrain models of star and planet formation. Clusters at ages of 1–5 Myr with space-based rotation measurements remain scarce.

Method: Rotation periods derived from K2 light curves. Analysis of 414 light curves yields reliable rotation periods for 325 stars (124 disk-bearing, 201 disk-free); period distribution examined vs. stellar mass and disk presence, and periodic variables identified.

Result: Reliable periods for 325 of 414 stars, substantially expanding the K2 rotation sample at 1–5 Myr. The distribution broadly matches expectations: slowest rotators at mid-K, with both lower and higher masses rotating faster on average. Weak evidence that disked stars rotate more slowly. A few complex periodic variables appear at surprisingly high mass compared with earlier examples.

Conclusion: K2 photometry provides a significant new sample of rotation periods for a ~1–2 Myr cluster, consistent with standard rotation expectations and offering a useful constraint for star and planet formation models, while revealing unusual high-mass periodic variables worthy of follow-up.

Abstract: A low-mass star's rotation rate drives its magnetic field, and in the case of young stars, also affects accretion and planetary formation. Observations of the rotation rates of young stars help constrain models of star and planet formation. We present rotation rates from K2 observations of stars in the ~1-2 Myr Lagoon Nebula, one of the youngest clusters with space-based rotation rates available. From 414 light curves we obtain reliable rotation periods for 325 stars; 124 of them are disk-bearing and 201 are disk-free. This constitutes a significant increase in the collection of K2 rotation rates available for ~1-5 Myr stars. The overall distribution is broadly consistent with expectations, in that the slowest rotators are mid-K and both lower and higher masses are on average faster rotating. There is weak evidence that disked stars rotate more slowly. There are a few complex periodic variables, but they appear to be surprisingly high mass compared to prior known examples of this kind of object.

### [3] [Enhanced Mass Transfer from Extended Shock-Supported Stellar Chromospheres](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29323)
*Soumyadeep Bhattacharjee,Jim Fuller,Kareem El-Badry*

Main category: astro-ph.SR

TL;DR: Shock-driven chromospheres substantially boost optically thin mass transfer before Roche lobe overflow, altering binary evolution outcomes.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Existing optically thin mass transfer models assume a tenuous thermally supported atmosphere, predicting negligible accretion until Roche lobe overflow. Recent hydrodynamic simulations of giant stars indicate shock-driven outflows create much thicker chromospheres extending to several stellar radii, motivating a physically grounded revision of pre-RLOF mass transfer rates.

Method: Derive a simple analytic model for a shock-supported chromosphere within a binary potential and compute the resulting mass transfer rate. Implement this prescription in the MESA stellar evolution code, and correct an inaccuracy in MESA's existing optically-thin Ritter (1988) prescription.

Result: Chromospheric mass transfer rates can exceed prior optically thin estimates by orders of magnitude, particularly for donors high on the red giant or asymptotic giant branch. This enables substantial accretion before Roche lobe overflow in both stable mass transfer and common envelope systems. The model resembles Wind Roche-lobe Overflow but is physically rather than empirically motivated.

Conclusion: Pre-RLOF chromospheric mass transfer can alter final orbital periods and component masses relative to classical predictions, with testable implications for post-interaction binaries via population synthesis and observed binary populations.

Abstract: Mass transfer (MT) in binary systems is a crucial and complex process. Even before the donor's photosphere fills its Roche lobe, matter from its outer atmosphere can reach the inner Lagrange point, thus initiating interaction in the so-called optically thin phase. Most existing models account for a very tenuous atmosphere supported by thermal gas pressure, thus predicting very little MT until the donor fills its Roche lobe. However, recent hydrodynamic simulations of giant stars suggest that shock-driven outflows can create substantially thicker "chromospheres'' extending to a few stellar radii. In this work, we derive a simple analytic model for a shock-supported chromosphere in a binary potential and calculate the corresponding MT rate. We also implement our prescription in the stellar evolution code MESA. We show that chromospheric MT rates can be orders of magnitude higher than prior estimates for optically thin MT, especially for stars high on the red giant or asymptotic giant branch. This allows for substantial accretion before Roche lobe overflow, both for systems that undergo stable mass transfer and for systems that go through common envelope evolution. Thus, for a given initial binary configuration, this may alter the final period and component masses compared to those predicted classically. Our model is similar to Wind Roche-lobe Overflow, but it is physically rather than empirically motivated. We discuss implications for various post-interaction binaries that can be tested with population synthesis studies and observed binary populations. Additionally, we have identified an inaccuracy in the current implementation of the optically-thin Ritter 1988 prescription in MESA, which we correct.

### [4] [simesh: A Python Framework for Quantitative Analysis of Native AMR Data from MPI-AMRVAC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29333)

TL;DR: simesh is a Python/Cython framework for quantitative analysis of cell-centered fields on native block-structured AMR meshes, and it shows that analyzing data on the original AMR hierarchy differs measurably from VTK point-data conversion or uniform-grid resampling, with discrepancies amplified in derivative quantities such as current density.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Large-scale astrophysical simulations rely on adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), but the resulting multilevel data are difficult to analyze quantitatively. Existing workflows often convert AMR data to point data (VTK) or resample onto uniform grids, which alters data association, interpolation support, and differentiation scale, potentially biasing derived quantities.

Method: The authors develop simesh, an independent Python framework targeting three-dimensional Cartesian datasets from MPI-AMRVAC and compatible AGILE snapshots. It preserves the native AMR hierarchy, combining Python workflow interfaces with compiled Cython kernels for efficiency. It integrates field diagnostics, tracing, regional statistics, and line-of-sight integration in one numerical representation. A comparison is performed between native AMR analysis and VTK point-data conversion / uniform-grid resampling.

Result: Comparison shows that changes in data association, interpolation support, and differentiation scale affect both sampled magnetic fields and derived current densities. Small differences in the sampled primary field become more pronounced in derivative-based quantities, particularly near coarse-fine interfaces.

Abstract: Adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) is widely used in large-scale astrophysical simulations, but the resulting multilevel data are challenging to analyze quantitatively. The framework combines Python workflow interfaces with compiled Cython kernels, supporting efficient analysis on AMR meshes. We compare native AMR analysis with a VTK point-data conversion and uniform-grid resampling, which shows that changes in data association, interpolation support, and differentiation scale affect both sampled magnetic fields and derived current densities. Small differences in the sampled primary field can become more pronounced in derivative-based quantities, particularly near coarse-fine interfaces. The applications combine field diagnostics, tracing, regional statistics, and line-of-sight integration on the same native AMR representation. simesh makes these operations available through reusable Python interfaces for reproducible analysis of multiscale simulation data.

*Jagdev Singh,R. Ramesh,V. Muthu Priyal*

TL;DR: A historical and forward-looking review of coronal spectroscopy, tracing it from 19th-century eclipse discoveries (helium, the 5303Å "coronium" line) to modern multi-wavelength observations including the VELC instrument on Aditya-L1, and outlining the need for future coronal spectroscopy.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Solar coronal spectroscopy has been central to unravelling the corona's composition and thermal/non-thermal state—from discovering helium and the mystery "coronium" line to establishing the corona's ~10^6 K temperature and the presence of non-thermal motions. A consolidated account of these developments is needed to motivate and guide future spectroscopic observations.

Method: Review of historical eclipse spectroscopy (1860 onward, incl. Janssen 1868 and Young/Harkness 1869), identification of the green line as [Fe XIV] (1939), analysis of emission-line widths and non-thermal velocities, and survey of spectroscopic observations across UV, EUV, X-ray, visible, infrared, and radio wavelengths, including current instruments such as VELC aboard Aditya-L1.

Result: The 5303Å green line was identified as [Fe XIV] in 1939, revealing the corona to be hot at ~10^6 K. High-resolution spectra show emission lines broader than expected, interpreted as non-thermal velocity, and recent observations reveal complex variations of line parameters with height.

Conclusion: Coronal spectroscopy has a rich legacy and has already transformed our understanding of the corona. Continued and expanded spectroscopic observations—using current facilities like VELC/Aditya-L1 and future instruments across multiple wavelengths—are essential for characterizing the corona's thermal and non-thermal structure.

Abstract: After the beginning of photography of solar corona during the eclipses in the year 1860, efforts began to carry out spectroscopic observations of solar corona. During the total solar eclipse of 1868 August 18 at Guntur, Madras state, the observation of emission line 5875Å by Janssen lead to the discovery of Helium. Next year, during the eclipse of 1869 August 7, Charles Young and William Harkness discovered green emission line around 5310Å (later measured as 5303Å). It was interpreted as due to Coronium, an unknown element. The identification of this green line in 1939 due to [Fe xiv] ion showed the corona to be hot at 10^6 K. High resolution spectroscopic observations indicated that width of emission lines to be more than expected. The excess line width was interpreted in terms of existence of non-thermal velocity. Recent spectroscopic observations have indicated a complex nature of variation of emission line parameters with height above the corona. The spectroscopy of solar corona is also performed in other wavelengths, such as UV, EUV, X-rays, visible, infrared, and radio part of the spectrum. Here, we discuss the past spectroscopic observations of solar corona, present observations with various instruments, such as Visible Emission Line Coronagraph onboard Aditya-L1, and need for the spectroscopic observations in future.
