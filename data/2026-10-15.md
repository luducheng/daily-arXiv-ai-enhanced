### [1] [Any colour you like: The diverse chromaticities of white dwarfs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18966)
*Mark A. Hollands,Boris T. Gaensicke,Pier-Emmanuel Tremblay,Detlev Koester*

Main category: astro-ph.SR

TL;DR: This paper investigates the perceived colours (chromaticity) of white dwarf stars, finding that extreme opacity broadening in ultra-dense atmospheres of some objects produces non-blackbody colours (yellow, green, cyan, pink, purple), while the population median correlated colour temperature of ~6800 K confirms white dwarfs are, on average, white.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Main sequence stars appear orange-to-white-to-blue with increasing effective temperature because their opacities deviate only slightly from blackbody colours across the human visual broadband. White dwarfs, however, can have far denser atmospheres (~1 g/cm³ for the coolest objects), and metals such as carbon, calcium, or sodium in these ultra-dense atmospheres can cause extreme broadening of atomic and molecular opacities, potentially producing optical spectra that diverge significantly from blackbodies. The authors therefore set out to determine how temperature and chemical composition combine to shape white dwarf perceived colours.

Method: The authors computed perceived colours (chromaticity) of white dwarfs by combining spectral models with a treatment of the combination of temperature and chemical composition, integrating the resulting spectra over the broadband sensitivity curves of the human visual system. They examined the chromaticity distribution for the volume-limited 20 pc local white dwarf sample, derived correlated colour temperatures, and generated colour-accurate images of stellar discs from the spectral models.

Result: Most white dwarfs are similar in colour to blackbodies. Nonetheless, the authors demonstrate known stars that would appear yellow, green, cyan, pink, or purple if their stellar discs were resolved at close range. For the volume-limited 20 pc sample, they found a median correlated colour temperature of ~6800 K.

Conclusion: White dwarfs are, on average, indeed white in colour, contrary to the exotic hues possible for individual metal-rich, ultra-dense objects. The authors further show that white dwarf spectral models can be used to generate colour-accurate images of their stellar discs as they would appear from up close.

Abstract: Main sequence stars have perceived colours ranging from orange to white to blue with increasing effective temperature. This is because their atmospheric opacities cause only minor deviations from blackbody colours when integrated over the broadband sensitivity curves of the human visual system. White dwarfs can have much denser atmospheres, reaching ~ 1 g/cm3 for the coolest known objects. If metals such as carbon, calcium, or sodium are present in these ultra-dense atmospheres, extreme broadening of atomic and molecular opacities can produce optical spectra that diverge significantly from blackbodies. We investigated how the combination of temperature and chemical composition can affect the perceived colours (chromaticity) of white dwarfs. While we found that most white dwarfs are similar in colour to blackbodies, we also demonstrate examples of known stars that would appear yellow, green, cyan, pink, or purple if viewed close enough for their stellar discs to be resolved. We examined the chromaticity distribution for the volume-limited 20 pc local sample of white dwarfs finding a median correlated colour temperature of ~= 6800 K, establishing that, on average, white dwarfs are indeed white in colour. Finally we show how white dwarf spectral models can be used to generate colour-accurate images of their stellar discs as they would appear from up close.
